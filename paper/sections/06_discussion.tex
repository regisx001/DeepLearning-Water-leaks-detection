\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Comparison with Naive Baselines}

To contextualize the CNN results, we compare against two naive baselines:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Always-Leak Baseline}: Predicts leak for every window. With 70 leak
    windows out of 108, this achieves 64.8\% accuracy, 64.8\% precision, 100\% recall,
    and 0.787 F1-score.
    \item \textbf{Pressure-Threshold Baseline}: Labels a window as leak if its mean
    pressure falls below a threshold (e.g., 2.5 bar). This simple rule captures many
    leaks but also flags normal low-pressure periods.
\end{itemize}

Our CNN achieves 71.3\% accuracy and 0.79 F1-score---modestly outperforming the
Always-Leak baseline. The CNN's key advantage lies in its \textbf{higher precision}
(74.1\% vs.\ 64.8\%), meaning it produces \textbf{fewer false alarms}.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Examining the 31 errors (21 false positives + 10 false negatives) reveals structural
patterns:

\textbf{False Positives (21 windows):} These errors occur primarily in Fold~5, where
Sensor S003 (zero leaks) is in the validation set. The model, trained predominantly on
leak-positive windows (65\% of training data), develops a bias toward predicting leaks.
This is a direct consequence of the \textbf{label amplification problem}.

\textbf{False Negatives (10 windows):} These occur in Fold~2 (S002, S007), where the
model fails to detect any leaks. S002 has only 1 leak window, and the leak pattern may
differ from what the model learned from the 8 training sensors.

\subsection{The Small Dataset Challenge}

The most significant finding of this study is not the final accuracy number, but the
\textbf{methodological insight} about working with small time-series datasets. Several
interacting challenges compound the difficulty:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Limited raw data}: 1,000 observations across 10 sensors yields only
    108 samples after windowing---far below the thousands typically used for CNN training.
    \item \textbf{Few groups for splitting}: With only 10 sensors, each fold contains
    only \textasciitilde21 windows, making metrics highly sensitive to individual predictions.
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous event distribution}: Some sensors have zero leaks (S003),
    others up to 5 (S010), distorting fold-level metrics.
    \item \textbf{Label amplification}: The ``any-leak'' windowing strategy inflates the
    positive class from 1.9\% to 64.8\%.
    \item \textbf{Overlap-induced redundancy}: Even with step\_size=5, consecutive windows
    share 90\% of their data, violating the i.i.d.\ assumption.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Lessons Learned}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Beware of inflated metrics}: Our initial model reported 99\% accuracy
    due to data leakage from overlapping windows.
    \item \textbf{Group-based splitting is essential}: For sensor data, standard
    stratified or random splits are insufficient.
    \item \textbf{Model complexity must match data size}: Our initial 3-layer CNN was
    replaced with a 2-layer architecture to avoid overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Aggregate metrics mask fold-level variance}: Per-fold analysis is
    essential for understanding model reliability.
    \item \textbf{Label amplification must be documented}: The transformation from 1.9\%
    (raw) to 64.8\% (windowed) fundamentally changes the problem.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Pre-screening tool}: The model's 85.7\% recall means it catches the
    majority of leaks, with false alarms filtered by a human operator.
    \item \textbf{Alerting system}: In real-time monitoring, imperfect alerts reduce
    mean time to detect a leak compared to no automated detection.
    \item \textbf{Data collection guidance}: Per-fold analysis identifies which sensors
    are well-modeled, guiding where to deploy additional sensors or collect more data.
\end{itemize}
