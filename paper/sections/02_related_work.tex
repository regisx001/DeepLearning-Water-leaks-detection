\section{Related Work}

Leak detection in water distribution systems has been studied extensively using both
model-based and data-driven approaches. This section reviews the main categories of
methods and positions our contribution within the existing literature.

\subsection{Model-Based Methods}

\textbf{Model-based methods} rely on hydraulic simulations and physical models of pipe
networks to identify anomalies. These approaches use fluid dynamics equations (e.g., the
Darcy--Weisbach equation for pressure loss) to compute expected sensor values, and flag
deviations as potential leaks. Transient-based methods inject pressure waves into the
network and analyze reflections to locate leaks. While accurate when the model is
well-calibrated, these methods require detailed knowledge of the \textbf{network
topology}, pipe materials, diameters, junction elevations, and real-time operating
conditions---information that is often unavailable, incomplete, or outdated, especially
in developing countries where infrastructure records are poorly maintained.

\subsection{Classical Machine Learning Approaches}

\textbf{Data-driven methods} have gained popularity due to their ability to learn
directly from sensor measurements without requiring explicit physical models. Traditional
machine learning approaches include:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machines (SVMs)}: used for binary classification of
    normal vs.\ leak states based on handcrafted features (mean, variance, slope of
    pressure signals). SVMs perform well with clear class boundaries but struggle with
    the subtle, overlapping distributions typical of early-stage leaks.
    \item \textbf{Random Forests and Gradient Boosting}: ensemble tree methods have been
    applied to leak detection using statistical features extracted over time windows.
    Their main advantage is interpretability---feature importance scores reveal which
    sensor signals are most informative.
    \item \textbf{k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)}: instance-based methods that classify new
    observations by similarity to known examples. While simple and effective for
    well-separated classes, kNN is sensitive to the choice of distance metric and
    suffers in high-dimensional feature spaces.
    \item \textbf{Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM}: unsupervised anomaly detection
    methods that learn the distribution of ``normal'' behavior and flag deviations.
    These are attractive when labeled leak data is scarce, but they cannot distinguish
    between different types of anomalies (leaks, sensor faults, demand surges).
\end{itemize}

These methods achieve reasonable performance but require significant \textbf{domain
expertise for feature engineering}---the practitioner must decide what statistical
summaries, frequency-domain features, or domain-specific indicators to compute before
training.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches}

\textbf{Deep learning approaches} have emerged as a promising alternative, as they can
automatically learn hierarchical features from raw sensor data:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)}: particularly \textbf{Long
    Short-Term Memory (LSTM)} networks, have been used for time-series anomaly detection
    in pipeline systems, leveraging their ability to model long-term temporal
    dependencies. However, LSTMs suffer from slow training due to their sequential
    nature and can be prone to vanishing gradients on very long sequences.
    \item \textbf{1D-CNNs}: have shown competitive or superior performance compared to
    RNNs for time-series classification tasks, with the added advantage of faster
    training and inference times due to their parallelizable architecture. The local
    receptive field of convolutional filters is well-matched to the localized temporal
    signatures of leak events.
    \item \textbf{Autoencoders}: both standard and variational autoencoders (VAEs) have
    been used in an unsupervised setting---the model learns to reconstruct normal
    behavior, and high reconstruction error signals an anomaly. This approach requires
    no labeled leak data but may produce high false-positive rates.
    \item \textbf{Transformer-based models}: attention mechanisms allow the model to
    focus on the most informative parts of a time series. While powerful, transformers
    typically require larger datasets to train effectively and may be overparameterized
    for small-scale problems.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Our work builds upon these advances by combining a multi-layer 1D-CNN architecture with
practical techniques for handling class imbalance and rigorous cross-validation, targeting
the specific challenge of detecting \textbf{early-stage leaks} that produce subtle signal
deviations. Unlike many existing studies that report results on a single train/test split
(often with inadvertent data leakage), we employ \textbf{sensor-wise GroupKFold
cross-validation} to provide honest, generalizable performance estimates.
