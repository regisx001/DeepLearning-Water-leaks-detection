\section{Methodology}

Our approach follows a pipeline consisting of five stages: sliding window creation, data
splitting, feature normalization, CNN model training, and cross-validated evaluation. In
this section, we describe each stage in detail and highlight the \textbf{methodological
pitfalls} we encountered and resolved during development.

\subsection{Sliding Window Transformation}

Raw time-series data is transformed into fixed-length \textbf{sliding windows} to provide
temporal context for classification. Each window captures $W = 50$ consecutive timesteps
across all three sensor features, producing input tensors of shape $(50 \times 3)$. At a
5-minute sampling interval, each window spans \textbf{4 hours and 10 minutes} of sensor data.

\subsubsection{Window Size Selection}

The choice of $W = 50$ is motivated by the temporal scale of leak events observed in the
data. Leak-induced pressure drops develop over periods of 30 minutes to several hours. A
window of 50 timesteps (\textasciitilde4.2 hours) provides sufficient context to capture
the onset, sustained deviation, and partial recovery of a leak event. Smaller windows
(e.g., $W = 10$) would capture only fragments of leak signatures, while larger windows
(e.g., $W = 100$) would exceed the total length of some sensors (95--112 rows).

\subsubsection{Step Size and Overlap}

Windows are generated \textbf{independently per sensor} with a step size of 5 (90\%
overlap). The window \textbf{labeling strategy} assigns a positive label (leak) if
\textbf{any timestep} within the window contains a leak event:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{window}} = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } \max(l_{t}, l_{t+1}, \ldots, l_{t+W-1}) > 0 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $l_i$ is the leak status at timestep $i$. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} shows
example windows for both normal and leak conditions.

single leak row can cause up to $\lceil W / \text{step} \rceil$ windows to be labeled as
positive. Table~\ref{tab:window_amplification} shows the per-sensor window distribution
after windowing.

\subsection{Data Splitting: Avoiding Data Leakage}

\subsubsection{The Data Leakage Problem}

A naive approach would be to pool all 108 windows and apply a \textbf{stratified random
split} (e.g., 80/20 train/test). This appears sound but introduces severe \textbf{data
leakage}: with 90\% window overlap (step\_size=5, window\_size=50), consecutive windows
share 45 out of 50 timesteps. During our initial development, this oversight led to a
model reporting \textbf{99\% accuracy and 100\% recall}---results that were entirely due
to data leakage.

\subsubsection{Sensor-Wise GroupKFold Split}

To eliminate data leakage, we employ \textbf{sensor-wise GroupKFold cross-validation}
(5 folds), where each fold holds out 2 sensors entirely. This guarantees that no sensor
appears in both training and validation, preventing the model from memorizing
sensor-specific patterns rather than learning generalizable leak signatures. Each fold
contains approximately \textbf{86--87 training windows} and \textbf{21--23 validation
windows}.

\subsection{Feature Normalization}

Each sensor feature is standardized to zero mean and unit variance using
\textbf{StandardScaler}. Crucially, the scaler is fitted \textbf{only on training data}
to prevent data leakage. A separate scaler is fitted for each cross-validation fold:

\begin{equation}
x'_j = \frac{x_j - \mu_j^{\text{train}}}{\sigma_j^{\text{train}}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu_j^{\text{train}}$ and $\sigma_j^{\text{train}}$ are the mean and
standard deviation of feature $j$ computed from the training set.

\subsection{CNN Architecture}

We employ a \textbf{1D Convolutional Neural Network} with two convolutional blocks
followed by a classification head. The architecture is deliberately compact to avoid
overfitting on the small training set (\textasciitilde86 windows per fold). Details are
given in Table~\ref{tab:architecture}.

\subsubsection{Architecture Design Rationale}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Two convolutional layers}: with only \textasciitilde86 training samples,
    a deeper network would overfit rapidly. Two layers with 32 and 64 filters provide
    sufficient capacity without excessive parameterization.
    \item \textbf{Batch Normalization}: stabilizes training by normalizing activations
    within each mini-batch, allowing higher learning rates and faster convergence.
    \item \textbf{Global Average Pooling} (instead of Flatten): reduces the feature map
    to a single vector by averaging across the temporal dimension, dramatically reducing
    the number of parameters and acting as a structural regularizer.
    \item \textbf{Moderate dropout rates} (0.3 and 0.2): provide regularization without
    being too aggressive on such a small dataset.
    \item \textbf{Kernel size 3}: captures local temporal patterns spanning 3 timesteps
    (15 minutes). The two stacked layers give an effective receptive field of 5 timesteps
    (25 minutes).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Strategy}

The model is compiled with the \textbf{Adam optimizer} (initial learning rate $= 10^{-3}$)
and \textbf{binary cross-entropy} loss. A batch size of \textbf{16} is used. To address
class imbalance, we apply \textbf{balanced class weights}:

\begin{equation}
w_c = \frac{N}{2 \cdot N_c}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N$ is the total number of training samples and $N_c$ is the number of
samples in class $c$.

Two callbacks are used:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{EarlyStopping}: monitors validation loss with patience of 15 epochs,
    restoring the best weights.
    \item \textbf{ReduceLROnPlateau}: reduces the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 when
    validation loss stagnates for 7 epochs, with a minimum learning rate of $10^{-6}$.
\end{itemize}

For each of the 5 GroupKFold folds, a \textbf{fresh model is trained from scratch} with
independent normalization to ensure complete independence between folds.

\subsection{Threshold Optimization}

For the cross-validation evaluation, we use the standard decision threshold of
\textbf{0.5}. Additionally, for the representative fold, we perform a \textbf{grid
search} over thresholds from 0.10 to 0.85 (step 0.05) to study the sensitivity of the
F1-score to the decision boundary.
