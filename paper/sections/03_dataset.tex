\section{Dataset}

The dataset used in this study consists of \textbf{1,000 time-series observations}
collected from \textbf{10 sensors} (S001--S010) deployed in a water distribution network.
Measurements are recorded at \textbf{5-minute intervals} over a period of approximately
\textbf{3.5 days} (January 1--4, 2024). Each observation contains three sensor signals
and two event labels, as described in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Dataset features and descriptions.}
\label{tab:dataset}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Feature}            & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Range}     \\
\midrule
Timestamp                   & Datetime      & 5-min intervals    \\
Sensor\_ID                  & Categorical   & S001--S010         \\
Pressure (bar)              & Continuous    & 2.4--3.9           \\
Flow Rate (L/s)             & Continuous    & 54--210            \\
Temperature (\textdegree C) & Continuous    & 10--25             \\
Leak Status                 & Binary        & 0 / 1              \\
Burst Status                & Binary        & 0 / 1              \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Per-Sensor Distribution}

The 10 sensors do not contribute equally to the dataset. Each sensor has between 95 and
112 observations, reflecting slight differences in recording start times or sampling
irregularities. More importantly, \textbf{leak and burst events are unevenly distributed}
across sensors, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:per_sensor}.

Several observations are noteworthy:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Sensor S003} has \textbf{zero leak events} but 3 bursts, making it a
    pure ``normal + burst'' sensor. This is important for evaluation: when S003 appears
    in a validation fold, the model only sees normal and burst windows, not leaks.
    \item \textbf{Sensor S010} has the highest number of leaks (5), contributing
    disproportionately to the positive class.
    \item \textbf{Leaks and bursts are mutually exclusive} in this dataset: no observation
    has both statuses simultaneously. This suggests they represent fundamentally different
    failure modes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Class Distribution and Imbalance}

The dataset exhibits a \textbf{significant class imbalance}: out of 1,000 observations,
only \textbf{19 (1.9\%)} are labeled as leak events, while the remaining
\textbf{981 (98.1\%)} are normal. This ratio of approximately \textbf{1:52}
(leak-to-normal) is representative of real-world scenarios where leaks are rare events.
Figure~\ref{fig:class_dist} illustrates this distribution.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/class_distribution.png}
\caption{Class distribution of the Leak Status target variable.}
\label{fig:class_dist}
\end{figure}

This extreme imbalance poses a major challenge for classification: a naive model that
predicts ``no leak'' for every observation would achieve 98.1\% accuracy while being
entirely useless for leak detection. This is why we use \textbf{balanced class weights}
and evaluate using \textbf{precision, recall, and F1-score} rather than accuracy alone.

in sensor signals rather than sharp, easily detectable spikes. Table~\ref{tab:feature_stats}

The most discriminative feature is \textbf{Pressure}: leak events show a mean pressure
drop of approximately \textbf{1.05 bar} compared to normal conditions (from 3.24 to

124.6 L/s), as water escaping through the leak increases the overall flow.
\textbf{Temperature} shows negligible difference between the two classes (17.9 vs.\ 17.4

\subsection{Leak vs.\ Burst Events}

The dataset contains two types of failure events: \textbf{leaks} (19 events) and
\textbf{bursts} (10 events). Understanding the difference is crucial for model design:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Leaks} are gradual, sustained events that cause moderate pressure drops
    and flow increases over extended periods. They are difficult to detect because their
    signatures overlap with normal operational variations.
    \item \textbf{Bursts} are sudden, catastrophic pipe failures that cause sharp,
    large-magnitude pressure drops and flow spikes. They are relatively easy to detect
    even with simple threshold-based rules.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/pressure_with_events.png}
\caption{Pressure signal with leak and burst events highlighted.}
\label{fig:pressure_events}
\end{figure}

In this study, we focus on \textbf{leak detection} (the harder problem) and treat burst
events as part of the ``normal'' class, since they are already detectable by simpler
methods and have fundamentally different signal characteristics.

\subsection{Temporal Patterns}

Analysis of the temporal distribution of leak events reveals that leaks are \textbf{not
concentrated in specific time periods} but spread across different hours of the day.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Isolated leaks}: Sensors S002, S004, S005, and S008 each have only a
    single leak event, making it difficult to learn recurring patterns.
    \item \textbf{Clustered leaks}: Sensor S010 has 5 leak events with temporal gaps
    ranging from 45 minutes to nearly 2 days. Sensor S009 has two leaks separated by
    only 15 minutes, effectively forming a continuous leak episode.
    \item \textbf{Widely spaced leaks}: Some sensors (S001, S006) have leaks separated
    by 8--48 hours, suggesting independent events.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Quality}

The dataset contains \textbf{no missing values} across all 7 columns and 1,000 rows. The
timestamps are consistent at 5-minute intervals within each sensor, though sensors have
slightly different start times and observation counts (95--112 rows). No duplicate records
or obvious data errors were detected during exploration.
