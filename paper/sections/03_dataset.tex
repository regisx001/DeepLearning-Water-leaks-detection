\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Dataset Description}
The dataset used in this study consists of 1{,}000 sensor readings
collected from a simulated water distribution network comprising
ten monitoring nodes (S001--S010). Measurements are recorded at
uniform 5-minute intervals spanning a 3.5-day observation period
from January 1 to January 4, 2024. Each record contains the
following fields:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Timestamp:} UTC datetime of the measurement.
    \item \textbf{Sensor\_ID:} identifier of the monitoring node
          (S001--S010), with approximately 99 readings per sensor.
    \item \textbf{Pressure (bar):} hydraulic pressure measured at
          the sensor node, ranging from 2.50 to 4.00 bar under
          normal conditions.
    \item \textbf{Flow Rate (L/s):} volumetric flow rate through
          the pipe segment, ranging from 50 to 210 L/s under
          normal conditions.
    \item \textbf{Temperature (°C):} water temperature at the
          measurement point (discarded, see Section~\ref{sec:tempremoval}).
    \item \textbf{Leak Status:} binary ground-truth label
          (0 = Normal, 1 = Leak).
    \item \textbf{Burst Status:} indicator for burst events,
          excluded from this study as it constitutes a distinct
          failure mode outside the scope of leak detection.
\end{itemize}

After removing the 10 burst-labeled records, the working dataset
contains \textbf{990 observations} with \textbf{19 confirmed leak
events}, yielding a class ratio of 98.1\% Normal to 1.9\% Leak.
This extreme imbalance is representative of real-world conditions
in healthy water networks and constitutes the primary modeling
challenge addressed in this work.

\subsection{Exploratory Analysis}

\subsubsection{Class Distribution and Label Quality}
No missing values were found in any column. Labels are mutually
exclusive: no record carries both Leak Status = 1 and Burst
Status = 1 simultaneously, confirming clean annotation. Leak
events are distributed across multiple sensors, with no single
sensor accounting for the majority of events, indicating that
leaks simulate realistic network-wide occurrences rather than
sensor-specific artifacts.

\subsubsection{Pressure}
Figure~\ref{fig:pressure_dist} shows the pressure distributions
for Normal and Leak classes. Leak events exhibit a mean pressure
of 2.19 bar compared to 3.26 bar for normal operating conditions,
a reduction of approximately 33\%. The standard deviation for
leak events (0.45 bar) is comparable to that of normal readings
(0.43 bar), indicating that the separation is driven by a shift
in mean rather than a change in variance. Notably, the lower tail
of the normal pressure distribution partially overlaps with the
leak range, confirming that pressure alone is insufficient for
reliable binary classification.

\subsubsection{Flow Rate}
Figure~\ref{fig:flow_dist} illustrates the flow rate distributions
by class. Leak events show a moderate increase in mean flow rate
(148.7 L/s) relative to normal conditions (124.2 L/s), consistent
with the hydraulic principle that a pressure drop at a leak point
drives increased upstream flow to compensate. However, the
distributions overlap substantially, reinforcing that no single
feature provides clean class separation and motivating the
multi-feature, temporal modeling approach.

\subsubsection{Temporal Behavior}
Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} shows the time-series of pressure and
flow rate for a representative sensor with confirmed leak events.
Red markers indicate timesteps labeled as leaks. Two observations
are critical: (1) the pressure signal exhibits a visible drop at
leak timesteps, and (2) the drop is preceded by a period of
relatively stable readings, suggesting that temporal context over
multiple consecutive readings is necessary to distinguish a
genuine leak from normal pressure variability. This motivates the
sliding window approach described in Section~\ref{sec:windowing}.

\subsubsection{Feature Correlation}
\label{sec:tempremoval}
Figure~\ref{fig:correlation} presents the Pearson correlation matrix
between all numeric features and the Leak Status label. Pressure
exhibits the strongest negative correlation with the leak label
($r = -0.31$), followed by Flow Rate ($r = +0.11$). Temperature
shows a near-zero correlation ($r < 0.02$) with no meaningful
discriminative signal. Temperature is therefore excluded from all
downstream modeling steps to reduce input dimensionality and
prevent noise injection into the feature representation.

    \centering

\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
Three features are engineered per sensor prior to windowing,
motivated by the hydraulic dynamics of leak events:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1}
    \label{eq:delta_p}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    Z_{F,t} = \frac{F_t - \mu_s}{\sigma_s + \varepsilon}
    \label{eq:flow_z}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    R_t = \frac{P_t}{F_t + \varepsilon}
    \label{eq:pf_ratio}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta P_t$ is the \textbf{pressure delta}, capturing the
rate of pressure change between consecutive readings of the same
sensor. $Z_{F,t}$ is the \textbf{per-sensor flow Z-score}, computed
using the mean $\mu_s$ and standard deviation $\sigma_s$ of each
individual sensor's flow history to normalize sensor-specific
baselines. $R_t$ is the \textbf{pressure-flow ratio}, which drops
sharply during leak events as pressure decreases and flow increases
simultaneously. The small constant $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ prevents
division by zero. These features increase the per-timestep feature
dimensionality from $d = 2$ (raw) to $d = 6$ (with engineered
features and sensor encoding).

\subsection{Sliding Window Construction}
\label{sec:windowing}
The dataset is restructured into fixed-length windows suitable for
deep learning input. Windows are constructed \textbf{per sensor}
after sorting records by \texttt{Sensor\_ID} and \texttt{Timestamp},
preventing cross-sensor temporal contamination. A window size of
$W = 20$ timesteps (100 minutes of sensor history) and a step
size of $S = 5$ (75\% overlap between consecutive windows) are
used. Each window is labeled by the \textbf{Leak Status of its
final timestep}, avoiding the inflated positive counts produced
by the ``any-leak-in-window'' labeling convention used in prior
work. The resulting dataset is partitioned into training (70\%),
validation (15\%), and test (15\%) sets using stratified sampling
to preserve the 1.9\% leak ratio across all three splits.
